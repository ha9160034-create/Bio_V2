# van Pinxteren & Jespers (2025) — Machine Learning in FEP Workflows

**Summary**: A review surveying the integration of machine learning into free energy perturbation, detailing active learning screening, neural network potentials, and deep-learning holo structure generation.
**Sources**: Integrating Machine Learning into Free Energy Perturbation Workflows.pdf
**Last updated**: 2026-11-01

---

## Citation and scope

Donald J. M. van Pinxteren and Willem Jespers, "Integrating Machine Learning into Free Energy Perturbation Workflows," *Journal of Chemical Information and Modeling* 65(20):9856–9864 (2025), doi:10.1021/acs.jcim.5c01449 (source: Integrating Machine Learning into Free Energy Perturbation Workflows.pdf).

The review synthesizes recent methodologies that merge Machine Learning (ML) and Deep Learning (DL) with rigorous Free Energy Perturbation (FEP) protocols. While FEP delivers benchmark accuracy ($\sim 1\text{ kcal/mol}$), its computational cost has historically confined its use to small congeneric libraries (tens to hundreds of molecules). The authors explore how ML extends FEP across four dimensions: active learning screening, structural starting-model generation, machine-learned interatomic potentials, and delta-learning corrections (source: Integrating Machine Learning into Free Energy Perturbation Workflows.pdf).

## Active learning for ultra-large library FEP screening

The authors evaluate Active Learning (AL) frameworks that deploy FEP as an automated, high-fidelity oracle within iterative screening cycles (source: Integrating Machine Learning into Free Energy Perturbation Workflows.pdf):
- **Iterative workflow**: An initial surrogate ML model (such as a Graph Neural Network, Random Forest, or Gaussian Process regressor) is trained on a small baseline of known affinities. The model predicts binding affinities and associated uncertainties across multi-million-compound virtual libraries.
- **Acquisition functions**: Compounds are prioritized for actual FEP calculation using Bayesian acquisition functions (e.g., Expected Improvement, Upper Confidence Bound) that balance exploitation (high predicted potency) with exploration (high model uncertainty).
- **Speedup and recall**: Benchmark studies across targets such as TYK2, PDE2A, and $A_{2B}$AR demonstrate that AL-guided FEP achieves up to **20-fold speedups** compared to exhaustive FEP screening, recovering the majority of top-tier nanomolar actives while computing alchemical free energies for only a tiny fraction of the library (source: Integrating Machine Learning into Free Energy Perturbation Workflows.pdf).

## Deep learning for complex starting structures

Alchemical perturbation requires an accurate initial three-dimensional receptor–ligand complex (source: Integrating Machine Learning into Free Energy Perturbation Workflows.pdf). The review surveys the deployment of modern biomolecular co-folding architectures:
- Co-folding models (including AlphaFold3, Chai-1, Boltz-1/Boltz-1x, and HelixFold3) generate multimolecular protein–ligand holo structures directly from sequences and SMILES strings.
- Benchmark evaluations (e.g., Furui & Ohue, 2025) demonstrate that when experimental X-ray crystal co-structures are missing, relaxed holo complexes generated by advanced DL models can provide viable starting points for relative FEP, bridging structure prediction directly into rigorous free-energy lead optimization (source: Integrating Machine Learning into Free Energy Perturbation Workflows.pdf).

## Machine learning potentials (MLPs) in FEP

A primary source of error in classical alchemical simulations is the reliance on empirical fixed-charge force fields (e.g., GAFF, OPLS, OpenFF), which fail to capture electronic polarization, charge transfer, and subtle torsional energetics (source: Integrating Machine Learning into Free Energy Perturbation Workflows.pdf):
- **Neural network potentials**: Emerging ML potentials—such as ANI-2x, MACE, and AIM-Net2—reproduce quantum chemical (QM) potential energy surfaces with orders-of-magnitude lower computational cost.
- **AIM-Net2 architecture**: Offers broad elemental coverage, supports both neutral and charged states, and combines machine-learned short-range interactions with physics-based long-range Coulomb electrostatics.
- **Hybrid ML/MM (DP/MM) simulations**: Software plugins now support dynamic partitioning into an ML region (the ligand and key catalytic pocket side chains) and a classical MM region (the bulk protein and explicit solvent). This enables QM-level torsional and electrostatic accuracy within alchemical FEP thermodynamic cycles (source: Integrating Machine Learning into Free Energy Perturbation Workflows.pdf).

## Delta-learning ($\Delta$-FEP)

The authors outline $\Delta$-learning strategies where machine learning models are trained on rapid, lower-level approximations (such as docking scores or MM-GBSA) and learn a mapping function ($\Delta_{\text{FEP}}$) calibrated against a sparse grid of true FEP calculations, correcting systematic empirical errors across congeneric chemical series (source: Integrating Machine Learning into Free Energy Perturbation Workflows.pdf).
